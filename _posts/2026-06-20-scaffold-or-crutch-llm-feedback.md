The promise of LLM-based study support is easy to state: every student gets
a patient, always-available coach. The risk is just as easy to state, and
much less discussed: **a coach that does the regulating for you is not
teaching you to regulate**.

## The self-regulation cycle, outsourced

Self-regulated learning theory describes a cycle — set goals, plan
strategies, monitor progress, reflect, adjust. Each phase is a skill, and
skills develop through practice. Now consider what a helpful LLM assistant
does when a student hands it an assignment brief: it sets the goals,
produces the plan, monitors nothing, and reflects on nothing. The output is
better; the cycle is skipped. The student got support *instead of*
regulation practice, not support *for* it.

This is the scaffold-versus-crutch question, and the metaphor matters:
scaffolding, in the original instructional sense, is support that is
**designed to be removed**. Most AI study tools have no removal plan.

## Design principles I am testing

In my prototype work, three principles distinguish scaffold-like from
crutch-like AI feedback:

- **Ask before telling.** The assistant prompts the student to state a plan
  before offering one, and responds to the student's plan rather than
  replacing it.
- **Feed back on process, not just product.** "You planned three sessions
  and worked in one, the night before" is feedback no essay-improver ever
  gives — and it targets exactly the skill procrastinators need.
- **Fade on schedule.** Support that was dense in week one should be sparse
  by week ten, with the fading visible to the student as a feature, not a
  degradation.

## The empirical question

Whether these principles actually preserve learning while keeping the
motivational benefits of AI support is an open empirical question — the one
my dissertation is designed to answer. The honest state of the field is
that we are deploying crutches at scale and calling them scaffolds. Before
AI feedback becomes the default study environment, we owe students an
answer grounded in evidence rather than product metaphors.
